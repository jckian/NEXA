## 1. What the site is

130 W. College Street sits in **Chinatown, Los Angeles**, on the block bounded roughly by North Spring
Street, North Main Street, College Street and Bruno Street — the flatter, more industrial eastern edge
of Chinatown, between the tourist/civic core (Broadway, Hill, Bamboo Lane, Central Plaza, Far East
Plaza, uphill to the west) and the Los Angeles State Historic Park / Metro right-of-way (to the east).
The **Metro A Line Chinatown station is directly adjacent** — the elevated station sits on Spring Street
*above College Street* [Wikipedia: Chinatown station].

The parcel's **current program is a surface parking lot** [Urbanize LA, Mar 17 2023; downtownla.com].

Per a search snippet of the project's CEQA Environmental Setting document, the **project site** is not a
single lot but an assemblage: **110, 114 and 130 W. College Street; 117 and 119 W. Bruno Street; and 945,
949, 953, 955, 959, 963 and 973 N. Main Street** — within the City's **Central City North Community Plan
area**, and identified in ZIMAS as being in a **Transit Priority Area (TPA)** per SB 743 [CEQAnet
Environmental Setting PDF, via search snippet — **not directly read**]. This assemblage is the single
most useful unverified fact in this dossier and should be confirmed first.

## 2. History — the succession spine (district resolution before 2023)

### 2.1 Rail and the "Cornfield" (1875–1901)

The land immediately east of this block was the **Southern Pacific River Station** (opened 1875; operating
c. 1876–1901), the "Ellis Island of Los Angeles" where new arrivals from the east disembarked. The rail
yard later went fallow as the "Cornfield" brownfield and is today **Los Angeles State Historic Park**
[Wikipedia: Los Angeles State Historic Park; California State Parks]. This is the reason the block's
grain is industrial rather than residential.

### 2.2 Old Chinatown, and its erasure by Union Station (1880s–1939)

**Old Chinatown** — centered on Alameda and Macy Streets, immediately south-east of this block — existed
from the 1880s until roughly **1933**, when most of it was destroyed to clear the site for **Union
Station** (opened **1939**). Hundreds of Chinese families were displaced [Wikipedia: Old Chinatown, Los
Angeles; NBC Los Angeles; California Historical Society]. This is the founding trauma of the district and
the reason "Chinatown" is *here*, north of where it was.

### 2.3 New Chinatown and China City (1937–1948)

Two competing replacement Chinatowns were built in the late 1930s, north of the cleared area:

- **New Chinatown / Central Plaza** (1938–present). In **1937** the Los Angeles Chinatown Project
  Association, galvanized by community leader **Peter Soo Hoo Sr.**, raised funds to acquire, design and
  build Central Plaza — a community-directed blend of Chinese and American architecture, with
  Hollywood set-design input, containing **Bamboo Lane, Gin Ling Way and Chung King Road** [Wikipedia:
  Chinatown, Los Angeles; LA Conservancy via Google Arts & Culture]. This is the tourist/retail core a few
  blocks uphill (west) of 130 College.
- **China City** (1938–1948), the rival, more explicitly theatrical development, which did not survive
  [Wikipedia: Chinatown, Los Angeles].

### 2.4 Far East Plaza and the postwar commercial layer (1976–)

**Far East Plaza** (built **1976**) became the district's second commercial anchor — an aging Chinatown
mall on Broadway [Discover Los Angeles; LA Downtown News].

### 2.5 The gallery wave (late 1990s–2010s)

From the **late 1990s**, vacant storefronts on **Chung King Road** were converted into art galleries —
China Art Objects Galleries (Giovanni Intra / Steve Hanson) is credited with putting Chinatown on the art
map; a further cluster launched on Bernard Street in **October 2003**. China Art Objects itself left for
Culver City in **2010**. Sociologist **Jan Lin**'s *Los Angeles Chinatown: Tourism, Gentrification, and
the Rise of an Ethnic Growth Machine* (2008) frames this as the district's gentrification inflection
[PBS SoCal Artbound; Wikipedia: Chung King Road; Lin 2008].

### 2.6 Transit arrives (2003)

The **Chinatown station opened July 26, 2003** as part of the original Gold Line (Pasadena) — now the
**A Line**. It is an **elevated light-rail station on Spring Street, above College Street**, with
pagoda-inspired platform canopies and the Metro Art installation *Wheels of Change* by Chusien Chang
(unveiled 2003) [Wikipedia: Chinatown station; Metro]. **This is the single most consequential event for
this parcel**: it converted an industrial back-block into transit-adjacent land.

### 2.7 Historic designation (2005)

**Central Plaza** was designated **City of Los Angeles Historic-Cultural Monument No. 826 in 2005** (the
East and West Gates specifically) [hmdb.org; LA Conservancy; Wikipedia: List of LA HCMs in Downtown].
**It does not cover 130 College Street** — Central Plaza is several blocks west. No historic status of any
kind was found for the 130 College parcel itself (which is, in any case, currently a parking lot).

### 2.8 The food-hall revival (2013–2016)

Far East Plaza was re-tenanted under **George Yu** (Macco Investments): Roy Choi's **Chego** (May 2013),
Scoops (2014), Andy Ricker's **Pok Pok Phat Thai** (2014), and **Howlin' Ray's (April 2016)**, which
became the emblem of "new Chinatown" food tourism — and of the displacement of older tenants
[LA Downtown News; Time Out; Discover LA; Reappropriate].

### 2.9 The park (2001–2017)

California State Parks acquired the Cornfield in **2001**; an interim 13-acre park opened **2006**; the
full **Los Angeles State Historic Park opened in April 2017** after a ~20-year process ($32M acquisition +
$20M development) [Wikipedia: LA State Historic Park; The Architect's Newspaper, Apr 2017]. It is the
site's principal green adjacency.

### 2.10 The housing wave, and the adjacent College Station saga (2018–)

**College Station** — a mixed-use project by **Atlas Capital Group** at **129–135 W. College Street and
924 N. Spring Street** — was **approved by the LA City Planning Commission in 2018**: reported as **725
market-rate units** (a full build-out figure of **770 units** above ~51,000 sf of ground-floor commercial
also appears in the DEIR-era description), with **none designated affordable** — a central grievance of the
LA Chinatown Community Land Trust and anti-displacement organizers [LA Planning College Station DEIR page;
LA Chinatown CLT; Urbanize LA; Apartments.com listing, now marketed as "Chinatown Station"].

> **Critical adjacency finding:** College Station is **across the street from** 130 W. College Street —
> **not on it**. Urbanize LA's March 2023 article on 130 College explicitly describes "Atlas Capital
> Group's 700+ apartment project **across the street**." The two are separate developments with separate
> owners on opposite sides of College Street. The address ranges are consistent with this (129–135 W.
> College vs. 110/114/130 W. College — odd vs. even side). **This is `reported`, inferred from one
> sentence plus address parity; it was not confirmed on a parcel map.** See §7.

### 2.11 The current proposal (2023–)

In **March 2023** the **Riboli family** (owners of San Antonio Winery, Lincoln Heights) announced plans to
redevelop the **surface parking lot at 130 W. College Street** as a **five-story creative-office
building**: ~**225,000 sf of office** above ~**8,200 sf of ground-floor commercial**, with a **440-car
garage**, designed by **Grimshaw Architects** as "three stacked massings that progressively step back from
Bruno Street as it rises," with a **mid-block central courtyard on Bruno Street** and a **plaza at Alameda
and College** [Urbanize LA, Mar 17 2023; Urbanize LA (initial-study follow-up); downtownla.com;
130college.com].

The project follows the Ribolis' other Chinatown investments, notably the **restoration of the Capitol
Milling Building across the street** as leasable offices [Urbanize LA, Mar 2023].

**Status:** in **environmental review** — an **initial study** has been published by LA City Planning; the
project **requires a general plan amendment and a zone change**. The initial study forecasts **construction
complete by 2028** [Urbanize LA, "Metro-adjacent office project moves forward…"]. (A downtownla.com listing
gives a 2026 completion — an older/stale figure; the 2028 initial-study forecast is the later and more
credible one. Both are `reported`; not reconciled.)

## 3. Regulation

- **Zoning:** **unknown.** ZIMAS was not queried (research cut short). The fact that the project
  **needs a general plan amendment and a zone change** is itself strong evidence that the current zoning
  and General Plan land-use designation **do not permit ~225,000 sf of office** at this scale on this
  parcel — but the actual zone string, height district and FAR were **not obtained**. Do not guess them.
- **Community plan:** reported as **Central City North Community Plan** area [CEQAnet Environmental
  Setting, via snippet]. The Central City North plan covers Chinatown, the Arts District, Little Tokyo and
  Victor Heights [LA City Planning]. **Whether Chinatown was folded into DTLA 2040 (adopted May 2023) or
  remains under the older Central City North plan was NOT resolved** — the planning.lacity.gov page fetch
  never completed. A "DTLA 2040 Chinatown plan summary" PDF exists (hcnnc.org), which suggests Chinatown
  *is* inside the DTLA 2040 study area, but this is **not confirmed**. This is the largest open regulatory
  question for the site.
- **TOC (Transit Oriented Communities):** the parcel is immediately adjacent to a rail station, which on
  its face implies a **high TOC tier (3 or 4)** — but TOC is a **housing** incentive program (density bonus
  in exchange for affordable units), and the current proposal is **office**, so TOC may be irrelevant to
  *this* project while being highly relevant to any residential alternative. **The tier was not looked up.**
  `estimated` at best; recorded as `unknown` in the JS rather than guessed.
- **Transit Priority Area (SB 743):** reported as **yes**, per ZIMAS as cited in the project's
  environmental setting [CEQAnet, via snippet]. TPA status matters: it removes vehicle-LOS from CEQA
  analysis and supports reduced parking.
- **Historic status (parcel):** **none found.** Central Plaza's **HCM No. 826 (2005)** covers Central
  Plaza, several blocks west — **not** this parcel [hmdb.org; Wikipedia].

## 4. Urban / transit

- **Chinatown A Line station: adjacent** — elevated, on Spring Street directly above College Street
  [Wikipedia: Chinatown station]. This is the defining urban fact of the parcel; the project markets
  itself as "adjacent to the Chinatown Metro Line Station" [130college.com].
- **Street hierarchy:** the site sits between **N. Spring Street** and **N. Main Street**, with **College
  Street** (east–west) and **Bruno Street** as the local frame, and a proposed plaza at **Alameda and
  College** [Urbanize LA]. Broadway and Hill Street — the retail/tourist spine with Central Plaza, Bamboo
  Lane and Far East Plaza — are uphill to the **west**; **Alameda** is the industrial/arterial edge to the
  **east**.
- **Los Angeles State Historic Park** is the major public open space in the immediate district [Wikipedia;
  CA State Parks].
- **Walkability:** rail-adjacent with a dense Chinatown street grid to the west, but an industrial,
  low-porosity block face on the parcel itself (currently parking) — `estimated`, from the sources'
  descriptions, not from a Walk Score lookup.
- **Adjacent development pipeline:** College Station (Atlas Capital, ~725–770 units) across College Street;
  **Redcar Properties' mass-timber office at 843 N. Spring Street**; **Homeboy Industries** to the north;
  Capitol Milling Building (Riboli, restored offices) across the street [Urbanize LA, Mar 2023].

## 5. Market / demographics (press- and census-tier)

- **Chinatown population: 7,798** (2020 U.S. Census), density ~19,230/sq mi [Wikipedia: Chinatown, Los
  Angeles, citing the 2020 census].
- Characterized as a **low-income, aging population**; secondary demographic aggregators report a median
  age of ~37 with ~14.6% aged 65+ [Wikipedia; Point2Homes/AreaVibes-tier aggregators — weak, `reported`].
  *Note the tension:* a neighborhood-level "average household income of ~$100,020 (2024)" also appears in
  aggregator data, which sits oddly against the "low-income" characterization — likely an artifact of new
  market-rate housing (e.g. College Station) shifting the average. **Not reconciled; treat both as soft.**
- **Displacement pressure** is the organizing political fact of the district: the gallery wave (late 1990s–
  2010s), the Far East Plaza food revival (2013–2016), and the market-rate housing wave (College Station,
  2018–) are read by organizers (LA Chinatown Community Land Trust; Central City United) as a continuous
  process; College Station's **zero affordable units** is the emblematic grievance [LA Chinatown CLT;
  Reappropriate; Lin 2008].
- **Office demand:** the 130 College proposal is a **speculative creative-office bet on a transit-adjacent
  DTLA-fringe site in a soft office market** — an `estimated` characterization on my part; the DTLA office
  vacancy data gathered for the City Market dossier (15.3% → 22.0%, Q1 2020 → Q2 2025 [CoStar / Avison
  Young]) is the relevant backdrop and is **not favorable** to this program. This tension is the most
  interesting thing about the site for scenario work.

## 6. Climate (light-touch, per scope)

- **Los Angeles State Historic Park** (opened 2017) is a substantial green/cooling adjacency — an unusual
  asset for an industrial-edge parcel [Wikipedia; CA State Parks].
- The parcel is **currently a surface parking lot**: locally maximal impervious/albedo-poor condition, i.e.
  a heat-island contributor in its present state, and therefore a site where *any* redevelopment with
  courtyard/plaza landscaping is a net-positive on heat — `estimated`, from the site's stated existing
  condition plus the project's stated open-space program, not from any measurement.
- No site-specific heat, flood, solar, wind or noise study was located. Regional UHI figures were **not**
  re-gathered for this site (the outage cut research short); the City Market dossier's regional figures
  apply to the LA basin generally but are **not** transferred here as if site-specific.
