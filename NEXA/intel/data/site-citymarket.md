## 1. History — the program-succession spine

### 1.1 Founding (1909)

The City Market of Los Angeles was founded in 1909 as a wholesale produce distribution market at 9th
Street and San Pedro Street, established by Chinese-American merchant **Louis Quan**, with the original
complex designed by the architecture firm **Morgan & Walls** — eight brick and reinforced-concrete
industrial buildings with Mission Revival-inspired towers and corner belvederes [CHSSC; LA Conservancy].
Formation followed infighting among shareholders and stall vendors at competing produce markets, which
split into two new markets in 1909; City Market obtained a 15-year lease on 6.7 acres from the Huntington
Land and Improvement Company for $230,000, later expanding three blocks south to 12th Street and one
block west to Wall Street [Gum Saan Journal search summary; CHSSC].

Ownership was explicitly multi-ethnic for its era: the incorporation (dated April 13, 1909, per one
source) is described as involving roughly 373 Chinese shareholders (~41% of $200,000 initial capital),
Caucasian shareholders (~41%), and Japanese shareholders (~18%) [CHSSC: chssc.org]. A second summary
(Gum Saan Journal, via search) gives slightly different share counts (158 Chinese holdings/40,885 shares,
65 Japanese holdings/35,150 shares, 19 Caucasian holdings) — the two accounts agree on the multi-ethnic
structure and Chinese-plurality capital but disagree on precise figures; both are **reported**, not
reconciled against the original 1909 Articles of Incorporation.

### 1.2 Operations, Chinese/Japanese produce economy (1910s–1950)

The market opened at 2am, earlier than competitors, and became the commercial anchor of what is
sometimes called the "Market Chinatown" district — described by LA Conservancy as "the largest collection
of pre-World War II commercial buildings associated with the Chinese community in Los Angeles." By the
1930s, Japanese-American farmers controlled roughly 15% of LA County's produce land but grew a
disproportionate share of the region's vegetables (reported 68% of vegetables, 85% of celery, 60% of
cauliflower) [CHSSC]. WWII incarceration of Japanese Americans (1942) temporarily removed that
competition; after the war the Chinese merchant community around City Market dispersed — driven by the
construction of China City and New Chinatown elsewhere and by improved housing access — leaving only an
estimated 25 Chinese families in the immediate area by 1952 [CHSSC].

### 1.3 Later 20th century and historic recognition (1992)

The former Community Redevelopment Agency identified the City Market and Market Chinatown District (by
1992, twenty structures: the eight original City Market buildings plus twelve nearby buildings) as
**eligible for listing in the National Register of Historic Places**, and it carries Culturally
Significant / California Register–eligible characterizations [LA Conservancy]. No evidence was found in
this session of a formal City of LA Historic-Cultural Monument (HCM) designation for the complex —
tagged **unknown**, should be confirmed against LADBS/OHR records.

### 1.4 End of produce operations, demolition, adaptive reuse (2009–2015)

Wholesale produce operations at City Market ended in **2009**; the parent company began repositioning
its real-estate holdings toward "other urban uses" [HansonLA: City Market South]. In **2012**, six of
the original 1909 buildings were demolished — LA Conservancy states this happened "before environmental
review began," making it a focal preservation controversy in the subsequent CEQA process [LA
Conservancy]. The redevelopment's environmental review was opened under **Case No. ENV-2012-3003-EIR**
(reported, LA City Planning EIR page, via search snippet — not directly verified). Around 2014, the
southern portion of the site began construction as **City Market South**: a 75,000 sf adaptive-reuse
project (vintage brick/bow-truss warehouses and open-plan concrete buildings on 2.5 acres) converted to
creative office, fashion showrooms, restaurants, and event space around a central plaza [HansonLA;
citymarketsouth.com]. This is the **currently operating program** on part of the site.

### 1.5 Master-plan entitlement saga (2013–2024)

The full 10-acre master plan was first publicly announced around **2013**: office, residential, hotel,
retail, and an educational campus, phased over an anticipated **25-year build-out** [Urbanize LA 2015].
City Council originally approved entitlements/a development agreement in **2018** [Urbanize LA 2022
summary]. In **June 2020**, Mayor Eric Garcetti **vetoed** that development agreement — the veto followed
corruption allegations against then-Council District 14 member José Huizar, who was later sentenced to
**13 years in prison (January 2024)** [Commercial Observer 2024]. A revised agreement was introduced by
Huizar's successor Kevin de León in 2022, replacing a $3.9M payment into the CD14 affordable-housing
trust fund with an on-site requirement of **10% affordable units** (reported as 94 total affordable units:
47 low-income at 50–80% AMI, 47 moderate-income at 80–120% AMI) [Urbanize LA, Aug 18 2022]. The **Los
Angeles City Council approved the revised development agreement on March 6, 2024**, granting a **20-year
period** to fulfill its terms; an exact construction schedule was not disclosed at approval [Commercial
Observer 2024].

### 1.6 Present state (as of this research, 260712)

City Market South continues operating as creative office/retail/event space on the southern portion of
the site. The remainder of the 10-acre master plan — up to ~1.7–1.9M gsf including ~945–948 residential
units, a 210-room hotel, ~225,000 sf retail, ~295,000–310,000+ sf commercial/office and educational space
— is **entitled but not confirmed under construction** as of the sources gathered here; no groundbreaking
or 2025/2026 construction-start announcement was found in this session [Urbanize LA 2015; downtownla.com;
Commercial Observer 2024]. Reported unit counts vary slightly by source and year (945 vs. 948) — flagged,
not reconciled.

## 2. Regulation

- **Current zoning (project parcel):** reported as **M2-2D** (Light Manufacturing, Height District 2,
  "D" limitation capping FAR at **3:1**) [LA City Planning EIR page, via search snippet — reported, not
  verified]. The project's entitlement requests a **Vesting Zone Change from M2-2D to C2-2** plus a
  Height District Change raising the "D" FAR limit to **4.1:1** [same source].
- **DTLA 2040 (Downtown Community Plan):** adopted by LA City Council **May 3, 2023**, replacing prior
  Downtown-area community plans through 2040 [CCALA statement, May 2023]. Under the new plan, most of the
  Fashion District (east of Stanford Ave, including the City Market blocks in general terms) is zoned
  **IX2**, permitting up to **4.5:1 FAR**, **no citywide maximum height limit**, but **prohibiting new
  housing and hotels** in "industrial" IX2 parcels [CCALA/Fashion District DTLA2040 neighborhood profile
  PDF]. **This directly conflicts, on its face, with the City Market master plan's housing/hotel program**
  — the likely resolution is that City Market's own project-specific vesting entitlement (C2-2, approved
  independently through its own EIR/zone-change process) supersedes or sits outside the IX2 default for
  this parcel, but this was **not confirmed against ZIMAS or the adopted DTLA 2040 zoning map** in this
  session. Tagged **unknown / needs verification**.
- **Historic status:** see §1.3 — eligible-for-National-Register district designation (1992), no
  confirmed HCM. Six of eight original buildings demolished 2012.
- **Adaptive Reuse Ordinance:** LA's Citywide Adaptive Reuse Ordinance (Ord. No. 188,793) was expanded
  citywide in 2024, but **LAMC Sec. 9.4.5 excludes the Downtown Community Plan Area** from the citywide
  program, substituting a separate Downtown Adaptive Reuse Program — meaning City Market's own reuse
  (City Market South) and any future conversions on this site fall under the DTLA-specific pathway, not
  the citywide ordinance [planning.lacity.gov/citywide-adaptive-reuse-ordinance; Bisnow].
- **Entitlement instrument:** Development Agreement, approved March 6, 2024, 20-year term [Commercial
  Observer].

## 3. Urban / transit

- Bounded by 9th, San Pedro, 12th, and San Julian Streets in the Fashion District, DTLA.
- **No rail station is directly on-site.** Nearest reported light-rail access is **Pico Station (A Line)**,
  ~20-minute walk; nearest subway is **Pershing Square Station (B/D Line)**, ~20-minute walk. Nearest
  transit generally is bus stop **9th/Maple**, ~3-minute walk [Moovit, via search].
- **Future:** the **Southeast Gateway Line** (formerly West Santa Ana Branch Transit Corridor) is planned
  to include a **Fashion District station near 8th & Los Angeles Streets**; Metro's target is roughly
  10 years to build, construction from ~2025, opening **~2035** [Urbanize LA; Wikipedia: Southeast Gateway
  Line]. This is the single most relevant future transit driver for the site's long-horizon build-out
  case, though the station is several blocks from the City Market parcel itself, not adjacent.
- The **Vermont Transit Corridor** (BRT, targeting 2028 opening ahead of the LA Olympics) runs along
  Vermont Ave, well west of the site — included here for regional context only, not a direct site driver.

## 4. Market / demographics (press-tier, tagged `reported`)

- DTLA residential population reported at ~90,000, having grown **+47% since 2010**, versus roughly flat
  growth for greater LA and a decline for LA County overall [downtownla.com-type market report, via
  search synthesis].
- DTLA multifamily occupancy reported around **95%**, indicating sustained housing demand even amid the
  office downturn [CoStar].
- DTLA office vacancy reported to have risen from **15.3% (Q1 2020) to 22.0% (Q2 2025)**, with average
  office sale price down ~27% over the same period; the city's policy response includes the 2024 Citywide
  Adaptive Reuse Ordinance [CoStar; Avison Young].
- Fashion District / creative industry: City Market South is positioned explicitly as a creative-office
  and fashion-showroom draw within the historic garment/apparel district [WWD; Digs.net].

## 5. Climate (light-touch per task scope)

- No site-specific heat/flood/topography study was located; all climate facts here are **citywide or
  regional**, not site-measured — tagged accordingly.
- LA's urban heat island effect is reported to raise paved/hardscape-heavy areas' daytime temperatures by
  as much as **+4°C (7.2°F)**, with localized hotspots up to **+12°C (22°F)** above rural surroundings;
  UHI hotspot area is projected to **expand by 2050** [Heat Island Group / MDPI Sustainability study /
  ArcGIS StoryMap]. Tree-canopy cooling in dense downtown blocks is reported to lower local temperatures
  by roughly **2–3°F**, and downtown LA's industrial-era, low-canopy blocks (which include the Fashion
  District) are flagged in equity research as under-canopied relative to wealthier LA neighborhoods
  [National Geographic; MDPI].
- The City Market master plan's proposed open-space program — an elevated linear park (~20 ft above
  street level) and two piazzas — is a **potential** heat-mitigation asset **if built as rendered**, but
  this is my own inference (**estimated**), not a stated climate commitment in any source found.
